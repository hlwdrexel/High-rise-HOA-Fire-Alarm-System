# Detailed Bill of Materials - High-Rise Fire Alarm System

## Project basis
- Building: 10 stories, 25 condo units per floor, 250 units total
- Unit layout: 2BR / 2Bath condo units, commonly 1-2 smoke detectors per unit plus common-area devices
- Design basis used for estimate: residential unit detection, corridor/hallway notification, stairwell and lobby coverage, fire alarm control panel, and emergency communications support
- Estimate basis: 2026 USD, distributor pricing, typical not-to-exceed pricing for a mid-sized high-rise residential installation; excludes permit fees, contractor overhead, labor, conduit, box fill, and site-specific code upgrades

## Budget summary
- Base equipment estimate: $128,000 to $188,000 USD
- Typical installed total (equipment + labor + commissioning): $220,000 to $340,000 USD

## Alternate Design Review

### 3A. Remote power sourced within each HOA unit with battery backup
- Each condominium unit receives a supervised local power source and local battery backup.
- This approach limits common floor distribution and keeps life-safety support close to the unit devices.
- Advantages:
  - Easier fault isolation and unit-by-unit maintenance
  - More direct accountability for unit equipment and battery upkeep
  - Lower dependence on shared floor electrical rooms
- Disadvantages:
  - More power supplies and batteries across the building
  - More maintenance points and battery replacement scheduling
  - Requires electrical feed or closet coordination at each unit or nearby service point

### 3B. Remote power sourced for each floor and distributed to units on that floor
- One supervised remote power cabinet is provided on each floor, with battery backup at the floor cabinet and local distribution to the units on that floor.
- This is often a more efficient solution for a high-rise with a common electrical service area on each level.
- Advantages:
  - Fewer power supplies and batteries than unit-by-unit architecture
  - Easier centralized maintenance at the floor electrical room
  - Well suited to a building-wide shared riser and floor distribution concept
- Disadvantages:
  - Requires dedicated floor electrical space and branch circuit coordination
  - Higher floor-level distribution wiring and terminations
  - Centralized backup can become a single point of failure if not properly sized and supervised

### Recommended design approach
- Use 3A when the HOA wants local accountability, unit-level supervision, and simpler troubleshooting at each dwelling.
- Use 3B when the building has secure floor electrical closets and the owner prefers centralized floor distribution.
- In either case, the remote power source must include a listed battery backup sized for the connected loads and supervised by the fire alarm system.

## Recommended procurement note
This is a typical 2026 budgetary estimate for a 250-unit high-rise residential fire alarm system using Honeywell NOTIFIER and System Sensor components. Pricing is based on distributor and project pricing for a mid-sized residential installation. For final approvals, request an updated quote from an authorized Honeywell/Notifier distributor or fire alarm integrator for the exact devices, cabinet configuration, code-required notification levels, and remote power option selected.
